Stars & planets · Space story 02

Inside a stellar nursery

Stars begin as clouds, gather through gravity, and spend millions or billions of years turning one kind of matter into another.

In a cold, dark region of space, a nebula may float for millions of years. The cloud is mostly hydrogen and helium, with a little dust. Gravity pulls dense patches closer together. As a patch shrinks, its center grows hotter until atoms begin to fuse and a young star switches on.

Different stars, different lives

Small stars burn their fuel slowly and can shine for a very long time. Massive stars burn brilliantly, then end in dramatic supernova explosions. Those explosions scatter elements such as carbon and iron. Dust from old stars later helps build planets—and us.

Why do stars twinkle?

Most stars shine steadily. They appear to twinkle because moving layers of air bend their light as it reaches our eyes. Astronauts outside Earth's atmosphere see the stars as steady points of light.

The big idea

Every element in the Universe has a history. The atoms in your bones and the food you eat have passed through stars or star-forming clouds long before Earth existed.
